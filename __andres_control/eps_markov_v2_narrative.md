## Why an IPW version 2

The cells above are version 1 of the EPS transition analysis and run unchanged. Their follow-up weights were built from the few variables that the transition engine itself uses, so the attrition model could not use what the panel knows about the people who left: housing, partner, work, smoking, depressive symptoms and self-rated health are all recorded at baseline and all predict both leaving the panel and drinking. Version 2 refits the attrition model with the agreed set of baseline predictors, documents in advance how deaths and impeded respondents are treated, and asks the one question that matters for the microsimulation: do the transition matrices move when the follow-up weights change?

Three things are new. First, the weight model is chosen once, before any resampling: a SuperLearner compares a handful of candidate learners by cross-validation on each interval and its ensemble prediction becomes the version 2 response probability. This pre-step is deliberately separated from the design replicates, so that the replicates measure sampling variation around a fixed weight model rather than re-selecting the model each time. Second, the matrices for the reference model A and for the lognormal model are estimated under three weighting schemes, official weight only, official weight times the version 1 IPW, and official weight times the version 2 IPW, with the same design replicates, and the differences between schemes are tabulated cell by cell. Third, the weights are judged by what they are for: covariate balance between the followed sample and the baseline risk set, the effective sample size they leave, and how much mass sits in a few extreme weights.

The transport section above already answers whether one interval predicts the other. What it does not show is whether the fitted models reproduce the observed destination shares within each interval and origin state; that table is added here from the held-out predictions of the version 1 cross-validation, with design-based intervals.

## Exits, attrition and the version 2 risk set

Deaths and impeded respondents are handled differently in the two versions, and the difference is recorded before anything is estimated. In version 1, the VI to VII interval is restricted to holders of the official longitudinal weight, which exists only for people re-interviewed in person at VII; the risk set is therefore already "survivors re-interviewed", deaths, impeded people and phone-only follow-ups never enter it, the official weight carries the attrition adjustment, and the extra IPW corrects only missing classification and invalid links. In the VII to VIII interval, version 1 removes the people listed in the VIII deceased file from the risk set but keeps those listed in the VIII impeded file, so the impeded count as attrition and the IPW treats them as missing at random given the covariates.

Version 2 treats deaths and impeded respondents as competing exits, not as attrition. They leave the risk set and the weight model in both intervals: people recorded as deceased or impeded at VII cannot enter the VI to VII interval anyway, because they hold no panel weight, and people recorded as deceased or impeded at VIII are removed from the VII to VIII interval. The attrition table reports them only as exit counts, so that the denominator is transparent, and the percentage followed is computed among the survivors who could have been followed. Respondents followed at VII only by telephone did answer the survey but not the alcohol module; they are "followed without the alcohol module" and count as a missing outcome, not as an exit. Mortality itself is not modelled here: it belongs to the mortality module of the microsimulation, which has its own cause-specific inputs.

The attrition table is reported by interval and origin state, with the C3 and C4 origins merged because the attrition model, unlike the transition model, does not need the sparse top category on its own. For each interval it lists the baseline respondents under the interval's weight rule (panel-weight holders for VI to VII, as in version 1; in-person VII respondents for VII to VIII), the deceased, the impeded, the phone-only follow-ups, the people interviewed with the alcohol module, those lost because they were not located or refused, and the percentage followed among survivors. Two features of the table follow from the weight rules rather than from the data: in the VI to VII interval the deceased, impeded and phone-only columns are zero by construction, because the baseline is the set of panel-weight holders, and the exits audit reports separately how many of the people in the VII exit files were VI respondents aged 18 to 65; and an "Unknown" origin row collects baseline respondents whose origin drinking state could not be classified, who are counted in the totals but excluded from the response model and the weights.

The predictors of the attrition model are measured at the origin wave. They are the variables agreed for this version, listed with their names in the public EPS files:

## Response model and SuperLearner weights

The response model is a survey-weighted logistic regression of "alcohol module observed at destination" on the predictors above, fitted separately in each interval with the published design (cluster and stratum identifiers, origin weight as the survey weight). It is reported as odds ratios with design-based 95% intervals. An odds ratio above one means that people with that attribute were more likely to be re-interviewed with the alcohol module than people at the reference level, holding the other predictors fixed; an interval that includes one means the data cannot tell the direction. The odds ratios of the "Missing" levels describe who left an item unanswered, not a substantive effect of the construct. This model is kept as the readable summary of who leaves the panel; it is not itself the version 2 weight.

The version 2 weight comes from a SuperLearner, which in plain words is a weighted average of several candidate prediction models, with the averaging weights chosen so that the combination predicts best on data it has not seen. Each interval's data are split into five folds, stratified so that every fold has the same share of followed and lost people; each candidate is fitted on four folds and scored on the fifth, and the cross-validated risk is the average of these out-of-fold scores. The ensemble coefficients are the non-negative weights, summing to one, that minimise the cross-validated risk of the combination; the discrete winner is the single candidate with the lowest risk. The table of risks per learner is the one to read: it says whether any candidate beats the trivial constant prediction, that is whether attrition is predictable at all from these covariates, and how far apart the candidates are. The ensemble coefficients are less informative, because when two learners make similar predictions the coefficients can shift between them without changing the combination.

The library has four members. SL.mean predicts the weighted follow-up rate for everyone and is the benchmark. SL.glm is the main-effects logistic regression, the same specification as the response model above. SL.glmnet is a penalised logistic regression that shrinks small coefficients towards zero. SL.ranger is a random forest of five hundred trees with a minimum node size of fifty, so that the trees describe groups of people rather than individuals; the tuning is recorded in the configuration table. All four are fitted with the origin weight as observation weight, so that the fit targets the weighted population rather than the sample. The SuperLearner is fitted once per interval with a fixed seed, before the design replicates, and its ensemble predictions become the version 2 response probabilities.

The probabilities enter the weights as in version 1: a floor at 0.02 so that no one is given more than fifty times their baseline weight, the completed weight (origin weight divided by the probability) is trimmed at its first and ninety-ninth weighted percentiles within each interval, and the trimmed weights are rescaled so that each interval keeps the mass of its observed pairs. The probability summary reports, per interval, the distribution of the probabilities and how many cases hit the floor and the trimming bounds.

## Three weighting schemes and the shift table

The matrices are estimated under three schemes, with the same version 2 risk set and the same fitting code. Scheme one uses the official weight only, normalised to the interval mass over the followed rows, and shows what the matrices look like with no follow-up adjustment beyond the one that the official panel weight already carries. Scheme two multiplies the official weight by the version 1 IPW, fitted with the version 1 function on the version 2 risk set. Scheme three multiplies the official weight by the version 2 IPW from the SuperLearner. Only the reference model A and the lognormal model are run; the other models were compared in version 1 and the question here is about weights, not models.

The intervals use the same JKn design replicates as version 1, with one convention that must be read with the results. Under scheme two the version 1 IPW is refitted inside every replicate, as before, so its intervals include the uncertainty of the weight model. Under scheme three the SuperLearner probabilities are held fixed and only the trimming bounds and the normalisation are recomputed in each replicate; those intervals are therefore conditional on the weight model, and they are slightly narrower than they would be if the SuperLearner were refitted with every replicate. Refitting it inside the replicates would be possible but expensive and would mix model selection with sampling variation, which the one-time pre-step was designed to avoid.

The shift table subtracts scheme one from scheme two and from scheme three, cell by cell, and reports per interval and model the mean absolute shift, the largest absolute shift and the cell where it occurs, by sex and age group, by sex and age group pooled over intervals, and over all. Whether a shift matters is not a question of statistical significance. A reasonable rule, proposed earlier for comparing models, is that two sets of transition probabilities are equivalent in practice when the prevalence they project for a subgroup differs by less than the confidence interval of the ENPG estimate for that same subgroup, since the ENPG is the survey that the microsimulation is calibrated to. A shift that stays inside that band changes nothing downstream even if it is larger than the design-based standard error; a shift in a high-consumption row that would move the projected C3 or C4 prevalence outside the band matters even if few pairs are involved. The relevant comparison for a shift is therefore the ENPG interval for the subgroup, not the log loss of the models.

## Balance and weight diagnostics

The purpose of a follow-up weight is to make the followed sample look like the baseline risk set on the predictors of attrition. The standardized mean difference measures this directly: for every predictor level it is the weighted mean among the followed people minus the mean in the full baseline risk set, divided by the pooled standard deviation, so that it is comparable across variables. Values below 0.1 in absolute value are the customary target for acceptable balance; values above it mark a predictor on which the followed sample still differs from the people it is supposed to represent. The diagnostic is computed for the three schemes, so the reader can see what scheme one leaves unbalanced, what the version 1 IPW repairs, and what the version 2 IPW adds.

The weight diagnostics describe the price paid for balance. The Kish effective sample size is the number of equally weighted observations that would give the same variance as the actual weights; the further it falls below the number of followed rows, the more variable the weights. The ratio of the maximum weight to the mean weight and the share of the total weight carried by the top one percent of rows show whether a few people dominate; the counts at the floor and at the lower and upper trimming bounds show how often the safeguards had to act. Balance obtained at the cost of a very small effective sample is not an improvement. One comparison needs care: the official-weight scheme is untrimmed, while both IPW schemes trim the completed weight at its first and ninety-ninth percentiles, which also cuts the tails of the official weight itself; a higher effective sample size under the IPW schemes therefore reflects the trimming and must not be read as a precision gain from the inverse-probability correction.

Both diagnostics are reported by interval and origin state as well as overall, because the transition matrix is estimated row by row: balance over the whole interval can hide a row, typically the heavier-drinking origins, where the followed sample is still unlike the people who left. It is in those rows that the three schemes may disagree most and where the shift table should be read together with the diagnostics.

## Temporal consistency within intervals

The transport section asks whether a model fitted on one interval predicts the other. The complementary question is whether the models reproduce, within each interval, the destination shares actually observed from each origin state. The version 1 cross-validation already produced held-out predictions for every pair, that is predictions made by a model that did not see that person; from them this section computes, for each interval, origin state and destination class, the observed share, the predicted share and their difference, with design-based intervals for the difference.
