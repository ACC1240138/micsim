## 1. Empirical tracking/stability of drinking participation, volume and HED over 1–10 years, by age and sex (panels)

### Takeaway
Adult drinking is moderately to highly stable over short intervals, but stability does not keep decaying toward zero over longer intervals. Correlations over 8–12 years still sit around 0.6–0.7 for consumption scales (ICC) and 0.3–0.75 for drinks/day. Stability is clearly lower in young adults (18–29), among heavy drinkers, and, for volume, among men. Annual multi-state Markov estimates from NESARC give 1-year "stay" probabilities of about 0.90 for non-drinkers and light drinkers, but only 0.32–0.52 for heavy categories. I found no published year-to-year correlations of drinking participation for the Latin American ageing panels (MHAS, ELSI-Brazil, CRELES).

### Cited Findings
**US general population (NESARC W1 2001–02 → W2 2004–05; ~3-year interval)**
- Puka et al. 2022 fitted a homogeneous continuous-time multi-state Markov model to NESARC W1–W2. Sample: 34,165 adults, mean age 45.1 (SD 17.3), 52% women. The 4 states were non-drinker (none in past 12 months), category I (women ≤20 g/day, men ≤40), category II (women 21–40, men 41–60) and category III (women ≥41, men ≥61). Covariates were age (7 levels), sex, race/ethnicity and education. 1-year probability of staying in the same state: non-drinker 90.1% (95% CI 89.7–90.5), category I 90.2% (89.9–90.5), category II 31.8% (29.7–33.9), category III 52.2% (46.0–58.5) — [Puka et al., Addiction 2022, doi:10.1111/add.16024](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- Same study, horizon-specific values: 10% of non-drinkers start drinking within 1 year and 36% within 5 years, driven mostly by younger adults. Category I drinkers: 6% (1 y) and 21% (5 y) stop drinking; 3% (1 y) and 10% (5 y) increase. Category III: 0.4% (1 y) and 9% (5 y) stop; 48% (1 y) and 71% (5 y) move to lower categories — [Puka et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- Same study, HED pattern states (none, occasional, monthly, weekly HED). Among non-drinkers, 99% (1 y) and 93% (5 y) still have no HED. Among drinkers without HED, 90% (1 y) and 78% (5 y) still have none. Among people with HED, 72–98% (1 y) and 42–63% (5 y) continue HED — [Puka et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- Same study, by group: women, older adults and non-Hispanic Other adults were less likely to move between states. Among heavier drinkers they were also less likely to move down. Lower education meant more movement overall but less exit from weekly HED — [Puka et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- Saitz et al. 2018, at-risk drinking in NESARC W1→W2 (2–4 years): 28% at-risk at T1. Of these, 73% were still at-risk at T2. Of the 72% who were not at-risk, 15% became at-risk. Age 18–20 was the strongest predictor of moving into at-risk use and of not leaving it. Age ≥65, female sex, non-white race/ethnicity and no AUD predicted moving to lower risk — [Saitz et al., J Subst Use 2018, doi:10.1080/14659891.2018.1497101](https://doi.org/10.1080/14659891.2018.1497101)
- Dawson et al. 2013: cessation among past-year ≥monthly drinkers in NESARC over 3 years (n = 14,885). Correlates of quitting differ by age: pregnancy, infant, drug use disorder at younger ages; income, volume and incident CVD at older ages. Smoking cessation (OR 2.8–3.5) and months since last drink are age-invariant correlates. Supports age-specific exit hazards and a "sick quitter" pathway — [Dawson et al., Addiction 2013, doi:10.1111/add.12079](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.12079)

**Three US panels (Kerr, Fillmore & Bostrom 2002)**
- Panels: NHANES I (3 measurements), Framingham Offspring (4) and NLSY (6). Correlations of monthly volume are high for adult samples measured ≤5 years apart, but low for longer follow-ups and younger samples. Heavy drinkers are much less stable than abstainers or moderate drinkers. Abstainers who later drink drink much less than the mean. The authors report that "an autoregressive, rather than Markov, model of consumption change is supported" and that subgroups move between abstention and light drinking — [Kerr et al., J Stud Alcohol 2002, doi:10.15288/jsa.2002.63.325](https://doi.org/10.15288/jsa.2002.63.325) (abstract only; full text blocked, so the exact correlation tables were not retrieved)

**Young adults**
- NLSY, ages 17–24 at baseline followed 6 years to ages 23–30: about 70% of men and 58% of women kept the same drinking status throughout. Onset of current and heavier drinking fell with age; offset rose with age — [Harford, Addiction 1993, doi:10.1111/j.1360-0443.1993.tb00811.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.1993.tb00811.x)
- Review: "maturing out" of binge and problem drinking begins around the transition to young adulthood and continues beyond it — [Lee & Sher, Alcohol Res 2018, doi:10.35946/arcr.v39.1.06](https://doi.org/10.35946/arcr.v39.1.06)
- Monitoring the Future: the age of peak binge-drinking prevalence rose across cohorts, from 20 (1976–85) to 22 (1996–2004) in women and from 21 to 23 in men. The decline in the late 20s is slower in recent cohorts — (search snippet of MTF young-adult literature; [PMC5553703](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5553703/); not opened)

**Sweden: stability by age and sex (two Stockholm cohorts)**
- PART cohort, ages 20–64, AUDIT score 1998–2000 → 2010 (~10–12 years), n = 4,277. Total ICC 0.69. Men 0.68, women 0.66. Men by baseline age: 20–29 0.62, 30–39 0.62, 40–49 0.66, 50–59 0.72, 60–64 0.81. Women: 20–29 0.59, 30–39 0.65, 40–49 0.68, 50–59 0.72, 60–64 0.70 — [BMC Public Health 2014;14:525, doi:10.1186/1471-2458-14-525](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-14-525)
- Stockholm Public Health Cohort, ages 18–84, normal weekly consumption in g/week, 2002 → 2010 (8 years), n = 16,688. Total ICC 0.62. Men 0.60, women 0.57. Men: 18–29 0.47, 30–39 0.53, 40–49 0.66, 50–59 0.61, 60–64 0.64, 65–74 0.62, 75–84 0.69. Women: 18–29 0.40, 30–39 0.56, 40–49 0.62, 50–59 0.59, 60–64 0.67, 65–74 0.62, 75–84 0.68. Young women 18–29 had the lowest stability and the largest mean decrease (−26%) — [same source](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-14-525)
- Same study, share "stable" (change within ±0.5 SD) over 8–12 years: 78% for AUDIT (PART) and 63% for weekly g (SPHC). Age 18–29 had OR 4.4–5.6 for a decrease versus the oldest group — [same source](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-14-525)

**UK: Whitehall II civil servants (baseline age 34–55; 7 phases 1985–2013; mean follow-up 19.1 years; 6,838 men, 3,372 women)**
- Within-subject change with age explained only 12.2% (men) and 10.0% (women) of variability in intake. Light-to-moderate baseline categories were largely stable over the life course. The heaviest baseline category declined by 47.0 g/week (men >250 g/week) and 16.8 g/week (women >100 g/week) per decade. This decline was not driven by sudden transitions to abstention. Trajectories converge toward moderate levels, and all drinker categories decline from about age 60–65 — [Knott, Bell & Britton, Addiction 2018, doi:10.1111/add.13949](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.13949)
- Nine UK cohorts pooled (59,397 people, 174,666 observations): mean volume in men peaks around age 25 at about 20 UK units/week, then declines and plateaus in mid-life, and declines again from about 60. Frequent (daily or most days) drinking becomes more common in mid to older age — [Britton et al., BMC Med 2015, doi:10.1186/s12916-015-0273-z](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-015-0273-z)

**Older adults only (flag: ages 50+)**
- US community sample aged 55–65 at baseline (n = 1,291), drinks/day measured at baseline and at 1, 4 and 10 years. Cross-wave correlations were r = 0.61–0.76 for women and 0.31–0.54 for men. Lighter drinkers: women 0.57–0.73, men 0.30–0.49. Heavier drinkers (above guidelines): women 0.20–0.54, men −0.07 to 0.34. The sample excluded lifetime abstainers — [Brennan, Schutte & Moos, Psychol Addict Behav 2010, doi:10.1037/a0018592](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018592)
- HRS, ages 51–61 in 1992, followed to 2006 (n = 6,787 with ≥5 interviews): average consumption declined, but individual rates of decline varied widely and a minority increased. A history of problem drinking predicted increases — [Platt, Sloan & Costanzo, J Stud Alcohol Drugs 2010, doi:10.15288/jsad.2010.71.169](https://doi.org/10.15288/jsad.2010.71.169)
- NHANES I Epidemiologic Follow-up, ages 25–74 at baseline (1971–74) with 3 follow-ups to 1992, n = 14,127: heavy drinking declined with age and tracked national per-capita consumption (period effect), with no cohort effect. The age-related decline was slower in men, married people and smokers — [Karlamangla et al., Addiction 2006, doi:10.1111/j.1360-0443.2005.01299.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2005.01299.x)
- ELSA (≥50 years): among people whose drinking changed, slightly more reported decreased frequency. Poor and deteriorating health was linked to a steeper decline in frequency — (search snippet; [PMC5426316](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5426316/); not opened)
- SHARE waves 1–8 (2004–2020), 8,998 employed adults aged ≥50 who retired during follow-up: near-daily, heavy weekly and monthly binge drinking change around retirement — (search snippet; [Am J Prev Med 2026, S0749379726002175](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749379726002175); numbers not retrieved)

**Australia (HILDA)**
- 16 waves (2001–2016): only 44% of respondents who called themselves lifetime abstainers in 2016 gave that answer in every earlier wave, 8% had earlier said ex-drinker, and 48% had earlier reported drinking (5% at risky levels) — [Callinan, Chikritzhs & Livingston, J Stud Alcohol Drugs 2019;80(5):552–556, PMID 31603757](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31603757/)

**Latin America**
- MHAS (Mexico; waves 2001, 2003, 2012, 2015, …): I found no published wave-to-wave drinking transitions or correlations. A Yucatán cohort aged ≥70 (2008–2010, 3 waves) reported current use of 11.7% → 10.5% → 9.9%, while "lifetime use" rose from 54.5% to 66.6%, an apparent reporting artefact — (search snippet; [PMC4850697](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4850697/); not opened)
- ELSI-Brazil: only cross-sectional alcohol analyses were found (2015–16: 29.4% current drinkers among 50+). No longitudinal transitions were found — (search snippet; not opened)

### Inferences
- Pattern relevant to the Chile model: stability rises with age. Over 8–12 years, ICC is about 0.4–0.6 at ages 18–39 and about 0.6–0.8 at ages 50–64 (Swedish cohorts). A single annual persistence parameter for ages 15–65 is therefore misspecified. Persistence should at least differ for 15–29 versus 30+.
- The Swedish 8–12-year ICCs (about 0.6–0.7) and the Whitehall finding that within-person change explains only about 10–12% of variance are far above what AR(1) with rho = 0.8 implies at those lags (0.8^8 = 0.17; 0.8^12 = 0.07). The Chilean EPS pattern r(7.7 y) = 0.478 > r(3.7)·r(4.0) = 0.333 is therefore typical, not an anomaly.
- Implied tetrachoric for NESARC at-risk drinking over about 3 years (my calculation from Saitz et al.: prevalence 0.28, persistence 0.73, incidence 0.15) is about 0.79. This is higher than EPS 50+ participation (0.57 at 3.7 y). The measures and populations differ (at-risk versus any drinking; all ages versus 50+; US versus Chile), so this is only a rough cross-check.
- Heavy categories are the least persistent but tend to regress toward moderate drinking, not abstention (Whitehall, Puka, Brennan). A latent-propensity model with a single correlation for both participation and volume may overstate exits to abstention among heavy drinkers. Consider separate persistence for participation versus volume among drinkers.
- Sex: evidence is mixed. Women's volume was more stable among older US adults (Brennan), but there were no major sex differences in the Swedish ICCs, and women were less likely to change states in NESARC (Puka). Treat sex-specific persistence as a sensitivity parameter, not a fixed rule.

### Gaps
- No published year-to-year or multi-year correlations or TPs for drinking participation in MHAS, ELSI-Brazil or CRELES were found. PSID, Add Health, NLSY97, BHPS/UKHLS, German SOEP and Nordic registries (HUNT, Tromsø) were not reached with retrievable numbers in this session.
- The full Kerr 2002 correlation tables (NHANES I, Framingham, NLSY by interval) could not be opened (jsad.com blocked).
- The Fillmore CARLP meta-analyses (41 longitudinal datasets, 15 countries) report cross-study life-course variation, but no stability coefficients were retrievable — ([Fillmore et al., Br J Addict 1991, doi:10.1111/j.1360-0443.1991.tb01702.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.1991.tb01702.x); abstract only).
- No published tetrachoric correlations of binary drinking status across waves were found for any panel. The EPS values remain the only direct evidence on the latent-propensity scale.

## 2. Model structures that reproduce slow decay (correlation not going to zero) and formulas linking parameters to the lag-d correlation

### Takeaway
A pure AR(1) forces corr(d) = rho^d → 0. The standard fix is to add a time-invariant person effect (random intercept / "stable trait") to an AR(1) state, with optional occasion-specific error. This is the STARTS / trait-state-error family (Kenny & Zautra 1995; RI-CLPM, Hamaker et al. 2015). It gives corr(d) = R·[λ + (1−λ)φ^d], with asymptote R·λ > 0. Fitted to the EPS 50+ tetrachorics, this gives about λ ≈ 0.45 and φ ≈ 0.69/year (latent 1-year correlation about 0.83). Alternatives are latent growth/trajectory models, mover-stayer or mixture Markov chains, hidden Markov models, and dynamic random-effects probit (state dependence plus heterogeneity).

### Cited Findings
- Kenny & Zautra 1995: a person's current standing comes from three variance sources: a term that does not change (trait), a term that changes (state) and a random term (error). The model is estimable with structural equation modeling from multiwave data — [Kenny & Zautra, J Consult Clin Psychol 1995, doi:10.1037/0022-006X.63.1.52](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.63.1.52)
- STARTS decomposes individual differences into a time-invariant stable component, a time-varying autoregressive component and an occasion-specific state component. ML estimation often fails (non-convergence, negative variances), and a Bayesian MCMC approach with weakly informative priors stabilizes estimates — [Lüdtke, Robitzsch & Wagner, Psychol Methods 2018, doi:10.1037/met0000155](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000155); bivariate extension in Stan with four-parameter beta priors on the AR effects — [Lüdtke et al., Multivariate Behav Res 2022, doi:10.1080/00273171.2022.2039585](https://doi.org/10.1080/00273171.2022.2039585)
- RI-CLPM: if stability is partly trait-like and time-invariant, autoregressive paths without random intercepts misrepresent within-person dynamics. Random intercepts separate stable between-person differences from the within-person (AR) process — [Hamaker, Kuiper & Grasman, Psychol Methods 2015, doi:10.1037/a0038889](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0038889)
- Meta-analytic stability-and-change model (MASC, built on trait-state models): stable influences account for 83% of reliable variance in personality traits and 42–56% for well-being measures. Stability of individual differences increases with age. This is an analogue, not alcohol data — [Anusic & Schimmack, J Pers Soc Psychol 2016, doi:10.1037/pspp0000066](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000066)
- Applications to alcohol: a trait-state-error model of alcohol use and problems in college students (N = 486, 12 assessments over 3 years) separated trait vulnerability from state-like covariation — [Read, Wardell & Colder, J Abnorm Psychol 2013, doi:10.1037/a0034918](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0034918). Drinking motives at ages 23.8, 28.9 and 33.5 were adequately modeled by a latent trait-state model with high rank-order stability — [Windle & Windle, Addict Behav 2018, doi:10.1016/j.addbeh.2018.03.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2018.03.002) (abstracts only; variance shares not retrieved)
- Kerr et al. 2002 report that "an autoregressive, rather than Markov, model of consumption change is supported" in three US panels. Their wording implies that past consumption beyond the last wave carries information, which is consistent with a person-level component — [Kerr et al. 2002, doi:10.15288/jsa.2002.63.325](https://doi.org/10.15288/jsa.2002.63.325)
- Growth-curve evidence: Whitehall II used random intercepts and slopes with an unstructured covariance. Trajectories differ by baseline level and converge with age — [Knott et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.13949). In the US 55–65 cohort, the unconditional linear growth model had intercept variance 3.25, slope variance 0.01 and residual within-person variance 1.24 (drinks/day scale) — [Brennan et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018592)
- Dynamic discrete-choice / dynamic random-effects probit separates true state dependence (lagged outcome) from unobserved heterogeneity (random effect). Results are sensitive to the treatment of initial conditions (Heckman/Wooldridge conditioning on the initial observation and on covariate means) — (search snippets: [Grotti & Cutuli, Stata J 2018, doi:10.1177/1536867X1801800406](https://doi.org/10.1177/1536867X1801800406); alcohol application with an ordered logit separating state dependence and heterogeneity: [NBER w31453](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31453); not opened)
- Duration dependence (a non-homogeneous Markov chain) appears in long-run data. In a treated-AUD population the annual probability of staying in very-high-risk drinking fell from 63% to 43% beyond 8 years, and the probability of moving from very-high-risk to abstinence rose from 7% to 20% — [Barbosa et al., Med Decis Making 2019, doi:10.1177/0272989X19873627](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X19873627)

### Inferences
Formulas. y_t is the latent standardized propensity; Var = 1.
- **Pure AR(1)** (current engine): Z_t = ρZ_{t−1} + √(1−ρ²)ε_t ⇒ corr(d) = ρ^d. With ρ = 0.8: 0.80 (1 y), 0.64 (2 y), 0.44 (3.7 y), 0.33 (5 y), 0.18 (7.7 y), 0.11 (10 y).
- **RI + AR(1)** (trait-state, no error): Z_t = √λ·T + √(1−λ)·A_t, with A_t = φA_{t−1} + √(1−φ²)u_t and T ~ N(0,1) fixed per person ⇒ corr(d) = λ + (1−λ)φ^d. Asymptote λ. Var(Z_t) = 1, so the prevalence thresholds of the current engine are unchanged. Setting λ = 0 recovers the current engine with ρ = φ.
- **STARTS** (trait + AR + state/error): y_t = T + A_t + S_t ⇒ corr(d) = (σ²_T + φ^d σ²_A)/(σ²_T + σ²_A + σ²_S) = R·[λ + (1−λ)φ^d], with R = (σ²_T+σ²_A)/total (reliability) and λ = σ²_T/(σ²_T+σ²_A). Derived from the decomposition in Kenny & Zautra 1995 and Lüdtke et al. 2018. The closed form was not checked against the original text, which was not opened.
- **Latent growth** (random intercept b0 and slope b1 in age): Cov(t, t+d) = σ²_0 + σ_01(2t+d) + σ²_1 t(t+d). The correlation depends on age t and need not approach a constant. Useful when trajectories fan out or converge, as in Whitehall.
- **Mover-stayer / mixture Markov**: a share s_k of "stayers" in state k never leave. P(in k after n | k at 0) = s_k + (1−s_k)[P^n]_kk, so long-run persistence floors at s_k. This is a discrete analogue of λ.
- **Continuous-time Markov (msm)**: P(t) = exp(Qt). A homogeneous chain gives geometric decay toward the stationary distribution. The "memory" of the initial state goes to 0 unless heterogeneity (covariates or latent classes) is added.
- Fit to EPS 50+ (assignment values; my calculation). Using r(≈3.85 y) = 0.577 (mean of 0.566 and 0.588) and r(7.7 y) = 0.478 with R = 1: λ = 0.448 and φ = 0.686/year. Implied corr: 1 y 0.83, 2 y 0.71, 5 y 0.53, 10 y 0.46, 20 y 0.45. If instead misclassification attenuates the tetrachorics (R = 0.9 or 0.8), the latent parameters become λ = 0.48 / 0.50 and φ = 0.74 / 0.81, with latent 1-year corr 0.86 / 0.91. Pure AR(1) annualized from each EPS interval gives ρ = 0.857 (3.7 y), 0.876 (4.0 y) and 0.909 (7.7 y). The implied ρ rises with lag, which is the signature of a trait component.
- Identification. Two distinct lags identify (λ, φ) if R = 1. Separating R also needs ≥3 well-spread lags. EPS lags 3.7 and 4.0 are nearly collinear, so R is weakly identified from EPS alone. Strategy: fix R from external reliability evidence (Section 5) and run a grid over (λ, φ). The existing local plan already proposes λ = 0.45 and φ = 0.65 as starting values ([plan_trabajo_post_reunion_ACC_2026-09-17.md](../../__andres_control/plan_trabajo_post_reunion_ACC_2026-09-17.md)), which is consistent with this fit.
- EPS is 50+ only. The Swedish and Kerr evidence implies lower λ (and/or φ) at ages 15–29. A defensible structure is λ(age), for example λ_young < λ_old, calibrated to ENPG age-specific shares of never and former drinkers (Section 4).

### Gaps
- No published alcohol study reporting STARTS or RI + AR(1) variance shares (λ, φ, R) for drinking participation or volume in a general adult population was retrieved. The trait-state alcohol applications found are in college or young-adult samples, and their numbers were not opened.
- The minimum number of waves for stable STARTS estimation is commonly given as ≥4. I did not verify this in a source here.
- No hidden Markov model application to population drinking with retrievable parameters was found. Shirley et al. 2010 (HMM for alcoholism treatment trials) was searched for but not retrieved.

## 3. How SIMAH and other alcohol microsimulations estimate transitions, annualize them, and validate persistence

### Takeaway
SIMAH has used two different approaches over time. (a) Puka et al. 2022: a continuous-time multi-state Markov model on NESARC W1–W2 (3-year gap), reported as annual TPs and externally validated against NESARC-III 11 years later (within 1–2.2 percentage points). (b) In the 2025 Lancet Public Health pricing model: ordinal logistic regression on pseudo-longitudinal BRFSS 2000–10, calibrated by Bayesian history matching to BRFSS 2011–15 and validated on BRFSS 2016–19. None of these papers validates individual-level persistence (autocorrelation). They validate population prevalence by category. The RTI AUD model (Barbosa 2019) annualized 3-year NESARC matrices with an approximate stochastic cube root and calibrated long-run (duration-dependent) TPs to a 16-year cohort.

### Cited Findings
**SIMAH — pricing paper (Kilian et al. 2025, the project's reference implementation)**
- Annual time steps, 2000–2019, synthetic US population aged 18–79. Education transitions come from Markov models on biennial PSID 2005–2019. "Changes in alcohol use categories are informed by ordinal logistic regression modelling of pseudo-longitudinal BRFSS data (2000–10)" — [Kilian et al., Lancet Public Health 2025, doi:10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00165-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00165-3)
- Calibration: a Bayesian framework in two steps. Estimated parameters (education TPs; alcohol ordinal-regression coefficients) are treated as priors. Posterior beliefs come from Bayesian history matching against ACS 2000–10 (step 1) and BRFSS 2011–15 (step 2), keeping parameter sets with mean implausibility < 3. Validation uses held-out ACS 2011–19 and BRFSS 2016–19. Uncertainty: 60 parameter combinations × 10 seeds = 600 runs — [Kilian et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00165-3)
- Global assumption (2) of the model: "the structure of the Markov and ordinal model used to inform transition processes in education and alcohol use, respectively, are reasonable representations of the corresponding population dynamics" — [Kilian et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00165-3)
- Policy step: participation elasticity first. Drinkers are sampled to quit in proportion to each category's probability of moving to abstinence, so category-1 drinkers are more likely to be chosen. Then beverage-specific consumption elasticities are applied, with a U-shaped individual elasticity–consumption correlation of 0.60 (sensitivity 0.8 and 0.4) — [Kilian et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00165-3)
- Study design paper: "Markov models will be used to inform transition intensities between levels of SES and drinking patterns" — [Probst et al., Am J Epidemiol 2023, doi:10.1093/aje/kwad018](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwad018) (abstract only)
- Protocol (technical review PDF, White Rose eprints): NESARC W1–W2 "will be used to inform transition probabilities between drinking patterns by subgroup". A continuous-time multistate Markov model on PSID is attributed to C. Buckley (unpublished manuscript, 2023) — (search snippet; [eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/198292](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/198292/); not opened). Code: Zenodo [10.5281/zenodo.15641639](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15641639), v0.1.1 (blocked here; per local note `p1kimi_Informe_escalamiento_APC_mortalidad_alcohol_Chile.md`, dated 2025-06-11).
- The same SIMAH microsimulation (2000–2030) was used for screening and brief intervention scenarios — [Lemp et al., JAMA Health Forum 2026, doi:10.1001/jamahealthforum.2026.2348](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamahealthforum.2026.2348) (abstract only; transition method not described there)

**SIMAH — NESARC Markov transitions (Puka et al. 2022)**
- The model is a homogeneous continuous-time multi-state Markov model. Instantaneous transitions are allowed only between adjacent states; direct jumps to non-drinker were tested and rejected by AIC. Age and education are time-varying (as reported at each wave); sex and race/ethnicity are fixed. Survey weights are handled by replicating observations, with CIs rescaled to the original n. R package version 1.6.9; the PMC text dropped the italicized package name, and it is presumably msm — [Puka et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- Model selection: a single "non-drinker" state (lifetime abstainers plus former drinkers merged) fit best. The authors note that merging may be necessary given inconsistent reporting of lifetime abstention, and that microsimulations can split the groups afterwards. A 4-category quantity model with 7 age groups was best by AIC — [Puka et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- External validation: annual TPs were applied 11 times to the NESARC I population (demographics held fixed). Predicted state shares differed from NESARC-III (2012–13, independent cross-section) by ≤1 percentage point for quantity and ≤2.2 for HED — [Puka et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- Stated limitations: only 2 time points 3 years apart. Markov models assume that time spent in a state does not affect transitions — [Puka et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)

**Earlier Sheffield/CAMH dynamic models (CASCADE)**
- CASCADEPOP builds the synthetic US population with iterative proportional fitting and includes alcohol-use patterns — [Brennan et al., Int J Microsimul 2020, doi:10.34196/ijm.00217](https://doi.org/10.34196/ijm.00217). The dual-process agent-based model, with daily decisions and habit formed from past drinking frequency, was calibrated to BRFSS 1984–2004. It fit prevalence and frequency well and quantity worse — [Buckley et al., Addict Behav 2021, doi:10.1016/j.addbeh.2021.107094](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2021.107094). In this family of models, persistence emerges from habit (past frequency) rather than from an estimated AR parameter.

**RTI long-term drinking model (AUD population; Barbosa et al. 2019)**
- Multinomial logit models per origin state on NESARC W1–W2 (n = 1,329 with lifetime AUD and treatment or informal care), with covariates age (3 levels), sex and race/ethnicity (24 groups), giving 3-year TPs among abstinent and WHO low, medium, high and very-high risk — [Barbosa et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X19873627)
- Annualization: "it is theoretically feasible to calculate 1-year probabilities by taking the stochastic cube root of the 3-year matrix … not all stochastic matrices … have real stochastic cube roots", so they searched with an optimization algorithm for a stochastic matrix close to the cube root. Resulting 1-year stay probabilities (mean of 24 groups): abstinent 0.93, low 0.81, medium 0.49, high 0.51, very high 0.63. Implied 5-year abstinent persistence is about 0.75 — [Barbosa et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X19873627)
- Long-run calibration: simulated annealing to a propensity-weighted 16-year California treatment cohort (years 3–8, n = 420; years 8–16, n = 378), giving three sets of TPs (years 0–3, 3–8, 8+). Validation used NESARC-III retrospective "period of heaviest drinking" versus current drinking (n = 1,244; 60% with ≥9 years between periods). Uncertainty was represented with Dirichlet-distributed TP rows — [Barbosa et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X19873627)

**Matrix-root methodology**
- Converting each transition probability separately with the rate formula is incorrect for multi-state models. The correct approach is the matrix root via eigendecomposition, with numerical approximations when it fails (no real stochastic root, negative entries) — [Chhatwal, Jayasuriya & Elbasha, Med Decis Making 2016, doi:10.1177/0272989X16656165](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X16656165)
- The msm package fits continuous-time Markov models to panel data observed at arbitrary times. It estimates the generator Q directly, so P(1 y) = exp(Q) is a valid stochastic matrix by construction — [Jackson, J Stat Softw 2011, doi:10.18637/jss.v038.i08](https://doi.org/10.18637/jss.v038.i08) (cited from prior knowledge; not opened here)

### Inferences
- SIMAH's validation targets are marginal category prevalences: NESARC-III in Puka, and BRFSS 2016–19 in Kilian. A homogeneous Markov chain or an ordinal model fitted to pseudo-longitudinal data can match marginals while implying unrealistic individual persistence. The Chile model therefore needs an explicit persistence target (EPS tetrachorics) in addition to ENPG prevalence targets. Matching ENPG marginals alone does not identify persistence.
- For EPS with unequal gaps (about 3.7–4.0 and 7.7 years), msm-style generator estimation is the natural tool: it handles irregular observation times, and exp(Q) gives valid annual matrices. A matrix p-th root of an empirical 4-year matrix may not exist or may have negative entries (Chhatwal 2016; Barbosa 2019). Avoid "α = 0.5 per probability" shortcuts.
- A first-order homogeneous Markov chain on observed states has the same "forgetting" problem as AR(1). Puka's model reproduces 11-year marginals, but its implied 7.7-year state-to-state association would still decay geometrically. Fitting EPS needs heterogeneity: covariates, a mover-stayer share, or a latent trait. This parallels the λ term in Section 2.
- Reference-model sequencing (AGENTS.md §3) is consistent with SIMAH: transitions or persistence come first in the annual cycle; policy then acts through participation (exit sampling weighted by each category's exit propensity) and then consumption. The persistence structure determines who is "close to quitting" when participation elasticity is applied. This is one more reason to get λ and φ right.

### Gaps
- The SIMAH supplementary appendix (pp. 3–22 of the Lancet PH supplement) describing how "pseudo-longitudinal BRFSS" was built (matching rule, ordinal model form, lag structure) could not be opened (thelancet.com and Zenodo blocked). The construction should be checked in the Zenodo release before the Chilean ENPG pseudo-panel (`jrt/simulacion/Alcohol Transitions FINAL.R`) claims equivalence.
- The PSID-based alcohol transition model attributed to Buckley (unpublished 2023) was not found as a published paper.
- SAPM/STAPM (Sheffield) dynamic alcohol transition documentation was not retrieved in this session.

## 4. Identifying dynamics from repeated cross-sections and retrospective items (pseudo-panels, APC, never/former-drinker targets)

### Takeaway
Repeated cross-sections identify cohort-level (pseudo-panel) means and transitions in aggregate, not individual autocorrelation, unless one adds structure (Moffitt-type Markov models with covariates, ecological inference). In alcohol microsimulation this is handled by calibration: SIMAH (pseudo-longitudinal BRFSS plus history matching) and RTI (retrospective NESARC-III items as validation). For Chile, cohort-specific shares of lifetime abstainers, former drinkers and age of onset in ENPG are informative calibration targets for persistence, because a low-persistence model generates too few never-drinkers and too many former drinkers by mid-life. But self-reported lifetime abstention is itself unreliable across waves (44% inconsistency in Denmark within 1 year; 48% in HILDA).

### Cited Findings
- Deaton 1985: a pseudo-panel of cohorts (groups sharing characteristics such as birth year) followed across a series of cross-sections allows fixed-effects estimation — (search snippet; [Deaton, J Econometrics 1985;30:109–126, doi:10.1016/0304-4076(85)90134-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-4076(85)90134-4)). Verbeek & Nijman 1993: Deaton's estimator is inconsistent when the number of periods is small, even with many cohorts — (search snippet; [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/r/eee/econom/v30y1985i1-2p109-126.html)). Verbeek & Vella 2005: pseudo-panel grouping is an instrumental-variables application, so standard IV conditions must hold — (search snippet; [J Econometrics 2005;127:83–102](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/econom/v127y2005i1p83-102.html))
- Moffitt 1993: dynamic models, including a binary Markov model with entry and exit probabilities, can be identified from repeated cross-sections using covariates — (search snippet; [J Econometrics 1993;59:99–123, doi:10.1016/0304-4076(93)90041-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-4076(93)90041-3))
- Pelzer, Eisinga & Franses 2002: a non-stationary heterogeneous Markov model estimates micro-level entry and exit TPs for a binary outcome from independent cross-sections, using a cross-level (ecological) inference approach. Validated by treating a 5-wave panel as independent cross-sections and comparing with panel estimates. Extensions use ML, parametric bootstrap and MCMC — (search snippet; [Political Analysis 2002;10(2):113–133, doi:10.1093/pan/10.2.113](https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/10.2.113); [working paper PDF](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/1687/feweco20010808163622.pdf))
- APC with repeated cross-sections for alcohol: Health Survey for England 2001–19 (about 227,900 respondents aged 13–85), with grouped and proxy-variable APC models for abstention and for consumption among drinkers — (search snippet; [Opazo Breton et al., Addiction 2024, doi:10.1111/add.16599](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16599)). General Lifestyle Survey 1984–2009: sex-specific APC effects on abstention and on drinkers' consumption (negative binomial) — (search snippet; [Meng et al. 2013, PMID 23941363](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23941363/))
- Panel-based APC: heavy drinking showed age and period effects and no cohort effect (NHANES I follow-up, 1971–1992) — [Karlamangla et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2005.01299.x)
- SIMAH used "pseudo-longitudinal BRFSS (2000–10)" with an ordinal model, then Bayesian history matching to later BRFSS waves — [Kilian et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00165-3)
- Retrospective items as targets: RTI validated long-run TPs against NESARC-III recall of an earlier heavier-drinking period. 32% of respondents had ≥20 years between periods. Unexpectedly, 66/1,244 reported moving from a lower to a higher state — [Barbosa et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X19873627)
- Lifetime-abstainer inconsistency: Denmark 2019 → 2020, 44.4% of self-declared lifetime abstainers in 2020 (n = 252) had reported drinking at some point in 2019. 39.7% contradicted earlier binge reports. 2.4% had a prior alcohol-related diagnosis in the register — [Ekholm, Bloomfield & Thygesen, Alcohol Clin Exp Res 2024, doi:10.1111/acer.15433](https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.15433). HILDA 2001–2016: 44% consistent, 48% earlier reported drinking — [Callinan et al. 2019, PMID 31603757](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31603757/)
- Selection into persistent non-drinking: British 1958 and 1970 birth cohorts. Persistent limiting long-standing illness since age 23 gave OR 4.50 (1.99–10.18) for being a lifetime abstainer at 33 and 7.02 (2.39–20.66) at 42 — [Ng Fat et al., J Epidemiol Community Health 2014, doi:10.1136/jech-2013-202576](https://doi.org/10.1136/jech-2013-202576)
- SIMAH/Puka merged lifetime abstainers and former drinkers into one "non-drinker" state for estimation (better fit; inconsistent reporting) and recommended that microsimulations split them afterwards — [Puka et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- Methodological note from US trend monitoring: repeated cross-sections show smooth aggregate change that "may mask individual change", and APC models are the standard tool to separate age, period and cohort in survey series — [Greenfield & Kerr, Alcohol Res Health 2003, PMID 15301398](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15301398/)

### Inferences
- What ENPG (2008–2024, ages 15–65) can and cannot identify:
  - Cohort-by-age prevalence of 30-day and 12-month drinking, lifetime use, and age of onset identifies the *net* flows. For example, the cumulative incidence of first drinking by birth cohort comes from lifetime use and age of onset. The share of former drinkers comes from lifetime yes, 12-month no.
  - It does not identify gross flows (in and out churn) or the autocorrelation.
- A latent-propensity engine has a testable implication. With a 12-month prevalence p(a) at each age and persistence (λ, φ), the model implies:
  - (i) the share of a birth cohort that has *ever* crossed the drinking threshold by age a, to compare with ENPG lifetime prevalence;
  - (ii) the share who drank before but not in the last 12 months, to compare with ENPG former drinkers.
  - Low persistence (pure AR(1), ρ = 0.8) maximizes churn, so it overproduces "ever drinkers" and former drinkers relative to observed lifetime-abstainer shares. These two targets therefore constrain λ and φ from cross-sectional data, which is the main lever for ages 15–49 where EPS gives no information.
- Calibration caveat: self-reported lifetime abstention is unreliable (about 44–48% inconsistency in Denmark and HILDA). Use a tolerance band or a misclassification term on the "never drinker" target rather than an exact match. Avoid fitting λ to the "never" share alone.
- 30-day versus 12-month items. A model in annual steps with a 12-month participation state should treat 30-day drinking as a sub-state or emission (drinkers with p(30d | 12m)). Otherwise intermittent drinkers inflate apparent churn.
- Age of onset gives a hazard of initiation by cohort, a direct estimate of entry transitions for ages 15–25 (a Moffitt-type entry probability). Recall bias in age of onset and lifetime use (see the MHAS and Yucatán lifetime-use artefact) argues for using onset as a soft target.
- A Pelzer–Eisinga–Franses or Moffitt binary Markov model on ENPG (participation by sex × age × cohort, with covariates) could estimate entry and exit probabilities by age. These are an alternative or complement to the latent AR, and can be checked against EPS for ages 50–65 where both sources overlap.

### Gaps
- No Chilean APC or pseudo-panel analysis of ENPG alcohol data was found in the literature; only SENDA trend reports were found (search snippets). Local scripts exist (`jrt/simulacion/Alcohol Transitions FINAL.R`, ENPG pseudo-panel plus ordinal transitions).
- No alcohol microsimulation was found that explicitly uses lifetime-abstainer and former-drinker shares by cohort as calibration targets for a persistence parameter. The proposal above is an inference, not an established practice.
- Deaton, Moffitt and Pelzer et al. were confirmed bibliographically, but their full texts were not opened.

## 5. Measurement error and its effect on estimated persistence

### Takeaway
Observed wave-to-wave correlations understate latent persistence because self-reports contain occasion-specific error and real short-term fluctuation. One-month test-retest reliabilities of consumption measures in general-population surveys range from about 0.65 (quantity) to about 0.85–0.91 (frequency and beverage-specific volume), and reliability is much lower among light drinkers (AUDIT 0.51). Under STARTS, corr_obs(d) = R·corr_latent(d). Ignoring R < 1 biases λ (the trait share) downward and makes the latent annual correlation look lower than it is. For a binary item with no time frame (EPS), misclassification of light or occasional drinkers is likely the main error source, and lifetime-abstention reports are especially inconsistent.

### Cited Findings
- General-population telephone survey retested 1 month apart (307 adult drinkers): test-retest reliability 0.65 for drinking quantities and 0.85 for frequencies. Low stability of quantity patterns, rather than unreliable reporting, explains much of the lower quantity reliability. "Ostensibly unreliable self-reports may be highly reliable but may reflect unstable drinking patterns" — [Gruenewald & Johnson, J Stud Alcohol 2006, doi:10.15288/jsa.2006.67.738](https://doi.org/10.15288/jsa.2006.67.738)
- US household survey, past-year drinkers aged ≥18 (n = 1,395), 2- and 4-week recall, alternate-forms and test-retest methods: reliability averaged 0.91 for beer, wine and spirits consumption measures — [Williams, Aitken & Malin, J Stud Alcohol 1985, doi:10.15288/jsa.1985.46.223](https://doi.org/10.15288/jsa.1985.46.223)
- AUDIT in a Swedish general-population sample (n = 457), retest about 1 month later: item correlations 0.6–0.8, total ICC 0.84, about 0.80 within sex, age and consumer strata, but 0.51 among low consumers. Kappa for AUDIT 8+ was 0.69 — [Selin, Alcohol Clin Exp Res 2003, doi:10.1097/01.ALC.0000085633.23230.4A](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.ALC.0000085633.23230.4A)
- AUDADIS-IV (NESARC) test-retest (n = 2,657 in subsets of about 400): reliability of alcohol consumption measures "good to excellent" — [Grant et al., Drug Alcohol Depend 2003, doi:10.1016/S0376-8716(03)00070-X](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0376-8716(03)00070-X) (abstract only; ICC values not retrieved). AUDADIS-5 (NESARC-III, n = 1,006): substance use disorder diagnoses and criteria scales "good to excellent" — [Grant et al., Drug Alcohol Depend 2015, doi:10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2014.11.026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2014.11.026)
- AUDIT-C in primary care (18,491 patients; screens 1–21 days apart): ICC 0.87; kappa 0.79 for the binary unhealthy-use indicator; ICC 0.93 online versus 0.81 in clinic — (search snippet; [Simon et al., Alcohol Clin Exp Res 2024, doi:10.1111/acer.15245](https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.15245); not opened)
- Lifetime-abstention reports are inconsistent within 1 year (Denmark, 44.4%) and over 16 years (HILDA, 48% had earlier reported drinking) — [Ekholm et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.15433); [Callinan et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31603757/)
- The trait-state-error framework explicitly separates error from state and trait. STARTS estimation is fragile, which motivates Bayesian priors on the variance shares — [Kenny & Zautra 1995](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.63.1.52); [Lüdtke et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000155)
- Kerr et al. 2002 conclude that baseline measures of drinking groups "are especially unreliable for younger samples, longer follow-ups and heavier drinkers" — [Kerr et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.15288/jsa.2002.63.325)
- Whitehall II authors note that convergence of heavy drinkers' trajectories may partly reflect regression to the mean rather than age-related change — [Knott et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.13949)

### Inferences
- Under classical error, corr_obs(d) = √(R_t R_{t+d}) · corr_latent(d). With equal R, the asymptote of the observed correlation is R·λ, not λ.
- Treating EPS tetrachorics as error-free gives λ ≈ 0.45 and φ ≈ 0.69 (latent 1-y corr ≈ 0.83). Allowing R = 0.8–0.9 gives λ ≈ 0.48–0.50, φ ≈ 0.74–0.81 and latent 1-y corr ≈ 0.86–0.91 (Section 2 calculation). For the simulation engine, which should reproduce *observed* survey correlations when compared with EPS, the right object is: latent engine with (λ, φ), plus an observation model with misclassification when computing simulated EPS-like correlations. Without that observation model, calibrating to observed tetrachorics underestimates true persistence.
- Thresholding a latent Gaussian at cell prevalence makes the tetrachoric of simulated binary states equal the latent correlation, before any misclassification. So the EPS tetrachorics map directly to the engine's latent corr(d) only if one assumes no misclassification of "drinks yes/no". The EPS item has no time frame, so occasional drinkers may answer inconsistently. This is likely the dominant error, consistent with AUDIT reliability of 0.51 among low consumers.
- Heavy drinkers' apparent instability (Kerr, Brennan, Puka) mixes true change, regression to the mean and error. Volume-category persistence (Puka's 1-year stay of 0.32–0.52 for categories II–III) should not be read as latent persistence of the propensity to drink heavily.
- For "never" versus "former" drinkers, recanting (people who report drinking at an earlier wave and lifetime abstention later) implies that observed lifetime-abstainer shares are biased upward relative to true never-drinkers. Persistence calibrated to them would be biased upward (too much λ). Use ranges, and possibly the share of former drinkers as the more informative target.

### Gaps
- No direct reliability (test-retest kappa or tetrachoric) of a binary "do you drink alcohol?" item without a time frame was found, either for EPS or comparable panels. This is the key unknown for setting R for the Chilean persistence calibration.
- Exact AUDADIS-IV consumption ICCs (NESARC) were not retrieved (full text not in PMC).
- No study was found that jointly estimates measurement error and trait/AR persistence for population drinking participation (a STARTS model on binary drinking).
