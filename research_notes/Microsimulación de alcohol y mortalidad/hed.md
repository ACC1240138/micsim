Scope note: compiled 2026-10-09. Sources marked "(local _bib copy)" were read in full from the project's encrypted `_bib/` bundles (decrypted to a temp dir, never into the repo); everything else comes from PubMed/PMC full text or abstracts, or from web-search summaries where flagged. ## 1. How do SAPM, SIMAH, OECD SPHeP-NCDs, WHO/GBD and InterMAHP/WHO CRA represent HED, and what functional forms link HED to volume, sex and age?

### Takeaway
No reference model uses a person-level logistic model of HED on volume with persistence. WHO CRA and InterMAHP take HED prevalence among drinkers as a survey-derived input by sex and age. They treat everyone above the binge threshold in average daily volume as HED by construction, and spread the remaining HED evenly below the threshold. SAPM v2 ties peak-day drinking deterministically to mean consumption. SIMAH in Kilian 2025 has no HED variable at all. The closest analogue to the Chilean design is a Dutch 2025 model, which fits a logistic HED model on weekly volume and then calibrates it.

### Cited Findings
- **WHO CRA / Shield 2025: what is modelled.** Consumption is modelled as APC plus drinking status plus past-30-day HED (≥60 g pure alcohol on one occasion) among current drinkers. Data come from 540 surveys in 174 countries. Drinking status uses a Dirichlet regression. HED among past-year drinkers uses a fractional response regression with these covariates: APC, GDP-PPP, GBD region, Muslim population share, prohibition, predicted abstainer prevalence, and sex × age (15–19 … ≥65). Two covariates absorb definitional differences: one for the HED threshold (reference ≥60 g/occasion) and one for the timeframe (reference past month or 28 days). — [Shield et al. 2025, Lancet Public Health, local _bib copy](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00174-4)
- **WHO CRA / Shield 2025: volume among drinkers.** Volume among current drinkers is a gamma distribution whose spread is predicted from the mean (Kehoe method). APC is multiplied by a 0.8 correction factor, and the upper integration limit is 150 g/day. — [Shield et al. 2025 appendix, local _bib copy](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00174-4)
- **WHO CRA / Shield 2025: global HED levels in 2019.** HED prevalence in the adult population was 16.7% (14.5–18.9): 23.5% in men and 9.7% in women. — [Shield et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00174-4)
- **WHO CRA / Shield 2025: authors' own caveats.** The authors expect HED to be underestimated "owing to populations excluded due to the design of the survey, participation bias, and social desirability bias". They also note that a binary HED measure ignores intensity and frequency. — [Shield et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00174-4)
- **WHO CRA: how HED splits drinkers in the AAF (Formula S5).** For IHD, ischaemic stroke and injuries, current drinkers are split into HED and non-HED. Below 60 g/day the integral has an HED part and a non-HED part. Above 60 g/day every drinker gets the HED risk function: ∫₆₀¹⁵⁰ P_CD(x)·RR_CD_HED(x)dx. In other words, average volume ≥60 g/day implies HED. — [Shield et al. 2025 appendix, local _bib copy](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00174-4); same structure in [Shield et al. 2020 appendix, local _bib copy](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(19)30231-2) and [WHO/Europe 2025 injuries web annex, local _bib copy](https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/383872)
- **Manthey 2019: HED from per-capita consumption.** HED prevalence (30-day, at least one occasion of 60 g among current drinkers) was estimated with fractional response regressions on surveys from 118 countries. Global adult HED was 18.5% in 1990 and 20% (17–24) in 2017, forecast at 23% in 2030. — [Manthey et al. 2019, Lancet](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)32744-2)
- **Kehoe 2012: gamma spread from the mean.** The gamma distribution fitted survey consumption better than the log-normal. The standard deviation is almost proportional to the mean: SD = 1.171 × mean for men (R² 0.947) and 1.258 × mean for women (R² 0.921). The data were 851 datasets from 66 countries. — [Kehoe et al. 2012, Popul Health Metr](https://doi.org/10.1186/1478-7954-10-6)
- **InterMAHP: inputs and threshold.** HED enters as an input, P_BD: the prevalence of binge drinkers in the *whole population* who drank at or above the binge level in the past month. The binge level c is user-defined and sex-specific; the Canadian example is 53.8 g for women and 67.3 g for men (4+/5+ drinks of 13.45 g). — [Sherk et al. 2017, InterMAHP v1.0 Comprehensive Guide, local _bib copy](https://www.uvic.ca/research/centres/cisur/assets/docs/intermahp-guide.pdf)
- **InterMAHP: splitting bingers by volume.** (1) Drinkers whose gamma-distributed average daily volume exceeds c are "guaranteed to be bingers" (P_BAT = ∫_c^z P(x)dx). (2) The remaining bingers, P_BD − P_BAT, are spread *proportionally* across the volume range below c. (3) If the gamma-implied P_BAT exceeds the survey P_BD, InterMAHP caps P_BAT at P_BD and deflates the integral. The guide says this happened only where drinker prevalence was very low. — [InterMAHP guide](https://www.uvic.ca/research/centres/cisur/assets/docs/intermahp-guide.pdf)
- **SAPM v2: binge proxy.** SAPM v2 uses peak-day consumption from the General Household Survey: the maximum drunk on any one day in the past 7 days. A "binge drinker" is anyone whose peak day exceeds twice the daily guideline, i.e. >64 g/day for men and >48 g/day for women (UK unit = 8 g). The modellers observed that "the probability and scale of peak/binge drinking is related to the mean weekly consumption". They fitted "linear binge models relating peak daily to mean consumption for each age/sex/drinker type group". Policy-induced changes in mean consumption then flow through these models into a revised peak-day distribution. No peak-day price elasticity could be estimated. — [Brennan et al. 2016, Ann Oper Res (PMC full text)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10479-013-1451-z)
- **SAPM v2: the authors' own limitation.** "Binge drinking would be better represented by a combination of both frequency of heavy drinking occasions and average consumption level on such occasions." — [Brennan et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10479-013-1451-z); full algebra in [Brennan et al. 2015, Health Econ](https://doi.org/10.1002/hec.3105) (not accessible here)
- **SAPM later versions: occasion-level patterns.** Hill-McManus et al. built "usual" occasion-based drinking patterns from 1,724 diary respondents (UK NDNS 2000/01). They reconstructed drinking occasions from blood-alcohol curves over time, summarised them as frequency, level and variation measures, and estimated each person's "usual" behaviour statistically. The stated purpose was risk modelling for acute harms. — [Hill-McManus et al. 2014, Drug Alcohol Depend (abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2013.09.022)
- **SIMAH (Kilian 2025): no HED variable.** Alcohol is represented only as average g/day plus WHO categories: abstainer; category 1 (≤20 g women / ≤40 g men); category 2 (20–40 / 40–60); category 3 (>40 / >60). Category transitions use an ordinal logistic regression on pseudo-longitudinal BRFSS (2000–10). Within a category, g/day is drawn from beta distributions fitted to BRFSS 2000–2014 for 92 strata (category × age × sex × race/ethnicity × education), and it is redrawn only when the category changes. Searching both the main text and the supplement found no HED, binge or occasion variable. — [Kilian et al. 2025, Lancet Public Health, PMC full text and local _bib supplement](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00165-3)
- **OECD SPHeP-NCDs (web-search summary only).** The alcohol module is documented as having two dimensions: g/day, and a drinking pattern (regular, binger, alcohol use disorder). Binge status comes from IHME data, with a binge defined as 60 g/occasion for men and 48 g for women. The earlier OECD CDP-alcohol model "randomly assigns drinkers to patterns of drinking based on probability distributions modelled as a function of age, gender, and quantity of alcohol drunk". — [SPHeP-NCDs documentation, alcohol page](http://oecdpublichealthexplorer.org/ncd-doc/risk_factors/alcohol.html); [Cecchini, Devaux, Sassi 2015, OECD Health WP 80](https://doi.org/10.1787/5js1qwkvx36d-en). Not verified against the primary text.
- **Dutch model (ten Dam et al. 2025): three stages.** This is the closest published analogue to the Chilean design. (1) A logistic model for being a drinker. (2) A negative-binomial model for weekly drinks (NABW − 1) among drinkers. (3) A logistic model for HED, defined as ≥6 drinks for men or ≥4 for women on one day at least weekly (10 g drinks). The HED model is conditional on NABW with interactions with calendar time, age, sex and education, plus age polynomials and step functions for survey trend breaks. Data: Dutch Health Survey 2008–2022, n = 107,535. — [ten Dam et al. 2025, PLOS ONE (PMC full text)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0327264)
- **Dutch model: shape of the fitted HED probability.** The conditional probability of HED rises with NABW and falls with age. It increased over calendar time in every subgroup, "especially … for persons who drink fewer alcoholic beverages per week". — [ten Dam et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0327264)

### Inferences
- There are three families of HED–volume linkage:
  - **Prevalence-as-input with a structural threshold rule** (WHO CRA, InterMAHP): HED probability is 1 if average volume ≥ threshold, and a constant below it chosen to match survey prevalence.
  - **Deterministic peak = f(mean)** (SAPM v2).
  - **Individual-level logistic on volume** (ten Dam 2025; OECD CDP as described).

  The Chilean logistic model on log(1+g/day) belongs to the third family. That family is the only one in which a survey-to-simulation mismatch in the volume distribution changes the simulated HED prevalence.
- InterMAHP's "proportional below threshold" rule assumes no volume gradient in HED below c. A logistic model on volume assumes a steep gradient. The truth lies between them, and the logistic slope is what pushes predicted probabilities to near zero for low-volume drinkers.
- From Kehoe's ratios, the implied gamma shape (mean/SD)² is about 0.73 for men and 0.63 for women. A volume distribution this skewed places most drinkers far below 60 g/day, so a volume-only logistic model has little mass to work with in the region where most HED reporters actually are.

### Gaps
- I could not open the SAPM v3/v4 technical reports or the full Hill-McManus paper (Sheffield and Wiley blocked). The exact regression forms (frequency, occasion size and SD as functions of mean weekly units, age and sex) are not verified.
- The exact OECD SPHeP-NCDs equations for assigning binge status, and whether binge status persists over time, are not verified.
- The GBD (IHME) treatment was checked only indirectly, through Shield 2025's statement that GBD bases injury outcomes on average volume. The current GBD documentation was not opened.
- Whether other SIMAH outputs (for example mortality modules) add HED beyond Kilian 2025 is not verified.

## 2. Joint distribution of volume and HED: HED among low-volume drinkers and among those with zero or missing usual quantity, and why volume-only models under-predict

### Takeaway
A large share of HED, both of HED drinkers and of episodes, occurs among drinkers whose average volume is low or moderate. Usual-quantity questions systematically leave out binge occasions. A model that assigns HED only as a smooth function of reported usual volume, and only to people with positive volume, therefore misses a structural segment of HED: low-volume or irregular heavy drinkers, and those whose quantity-frequency volume is zero or missing.

### Cited Findings
- **Naimi 2003 (BRFSS 1993–2001, ≥5 drinks per occasion).** 47% of binge episodes occurred among otherwise moderate (non-heavy) drinkers, and 73% of binge drinkers were moderate drinkers. Men accounted for 81% of episodes. Although episode rates peaked at ages 18–25, 69% of episodes occurred at ages 26 and over. — [Naimi et al. 2003, JAMA](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.289.1.70)
- **Esser 2014 (NSDUH 2009–2011, n = 138,100).** Alcohol dependence affected 10.5% of binge drinkers and 1.3% of non-binge drinkers. 90% of excessive drinkers were not dependent. — [Esser et al. 2014, Prev Chronic Dis](https://doi.org/10.5888/pcd11.140329)
- **Stahre 2006: binge drinks are missing from QF volume.** Respondents "do not typically include binge drinking occasions in estimates of their 'usual' or 'average' daily alcohol consumption". In BRFSS, adding binge drinks to quantity-frequency volume raised heavy-drinking prevalence by 19–42% in relative terms across all adults, and by 53% among binge drinkers. Half of women binge drinkers and half of binge drinkers aged ≥55 then met heavy-drinking criteria. — [Stahre et al. 2006, Addiction](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2006.01615.x)
- **Chile: the Stahre correction applied to AUDIT items.** Castillo-Carniglia applied it to Chilean survey data: V = (F1 − F2) × Q + 6 × F2. Here F1 = drinking days per month, F2 = days with 6+ drinks (AUDIT item 3) and Q = typical drinks per drinking day (AUDIT item 2). The result is multiplied by 13 or 16 g. Survey coverage of APC was only 17.7% (13 g/drink) to 21.8% (16 g/drink). — [Castillo-Carniglia 2013 doctoral thesis, local _bib copy; article: Castillo-Carniglia, Kaufman, Pino 2013, Alcohol Alcohol](https://doi.org/10.1093/alcalc/agt066)
- **Gmel 2011: RSOD is a mixture.** The effects usually ascribed to risky single-occasion drinking "may often be the effects of an undifferentiated mixture of risky single occasions and regular heavy volume drinking". The extra risk from RSOD is therefore mis-specified: underestimated for injuries and CHD, overstated for some chronic outcomes. — [Gmel, Kuntsche, Rehm 2011, Addiction](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2010.03167.x)
- **Holahan 2025 (MIDUS).** Among 1,102 US moderate drinkers (≤7 drinks/week for women, ≤14 for men), being a binge drinker (≥5 drinks) at baseline raised the odds of binge drinking 9 years later more than 6-fold. — [Holahan et al. 2025, Subst Use Misuse](https://doi.org/10.1080/10826084.2025.2570882)
- **ten Dam 2025: inconsistent reports.** In the Dutch data, 0.4% of respondents had a weekly volume that logically implies HED (≥42 drinks for men, ≥28 for women) but reported no HED. They were dropped from the HED model fit. — [ten Dam et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0327264)
- **ten Dam 2025: the chained model mis-predicts.** HED predicted through the fitted negative-binomial weekly distribution, before calibration, came out at 10.0% against 8.8% observed (2014–2022 adults). The authors attribute this to the negative-binomial distribution not being "flexible enough to reflect the data". — [ten Dam et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0327264)
- **Shield 2025: survey under-capture.** The authors expect survey-based HED to be underestimated because of survey design exclusions, participation bias and social desirability bias. — [Shield et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00174-4)

### Inferences
- **Why the in-sample intercepts are not the problem.** A maximum-likelihood logistic model with a full set of sex × age-group intercepts reproduces each stratum's observed HED prevalence *in the fitting sample* by construction (the score equation for the intercepts). So a −4.5 pp gap in the simulation must come from one or more of the following:
  - **Denominator mismatch.** HED reporters with zero or missing usual quantity are excluded from the "drinkers with positive volume" pool, but they count in the survey target among current drinkers.
  - **Volume-distribution mismatch.** The logistic model is evaluated on simulated volume that differs from the survey volume it was fitted on. Examples are a gamma or beta re-draw, upshifting or rescaling to APC, or a different g/drink. ten Dam 2025 shows the size of this effect (+1.2 pp in their case); the sign depends on the shape of the mismatch.
  - **Weights.** The model was fitted unweighted but is compared with weighted targets, or the reverse.
  - **Evaluation at the mean.** If p is computed at group-mean log volume rather than per individual, Jensen's inequality biases it.
  - **The persistence mechanism.** If the latent propensity is compared with a probability that is recomputed on updated volume, HED can be lost in one direction only. This happens, for example, when HED is kept only if volume stays high.
- The Stahre formula offers a Chile-ready, auditable fix for the denominator and volume problems together. Recompute volume including binge days, using AUDIT item 3 or ENPG 5+/4+ days, *before* fitting the HED model. Then HED reporters with zero or near-zero quantity-frequency volume enter the positive-volume pool with a volume consistent with their binges.
- Because most HED drinkers are moderate-volume drinkers (73% in Naimi), a logistic model whose slope on log volume is steep will put most of its predicted HED mass on heavy drinkers. Any compression of the upper tail in simulation then cuts HED prevalence noticeably.

### Gaps
- I found no published estimate of the share of HED reporters with zero or missing usual quantity in ENPG or any Chilean survey. This has to be computed from the microdata (`_enpg` bundle).
- I found no study that quantifies the bias in under-predicted HED prevalence specifically caused by fitting HED only on volume. The evidence is indirect: Naimi, Stahre, ten Dam.

## 3. Calibration methods and validation results (intercept or offset calibration to prevalence by stratum, quantile mapping, copulas)

### Takeaway
The published approaches fall into four groups:
- **Take HED prevalence directly as an input** and enforce consistency with volume (WHO CRA, InterMAHP).
- **Calibrate the volume distribution** so that the downstream HED logistic model hits the observed prevalence (ten Dam 2025, error <0.1 pp).
- **Bayesian history matching on category prevalences** (SIMAH).
- **Rank-based (quantile) matching** of synthetic individuals to survey respondents (SIMAH's "deterministic population").

No alcohol microsimulation using a copula for the volume–HED link was found.

### Cited Findings
- **ten Dam 2025: calibrating the volume distribution.** The negative-binomial mean and dispersion were scaled separately by sex (κ_s, λ_s) with numerical optimisation, minimising squared relative differences between predicted and observed prevalence. One calibration targeted excessive drinking and another HED. The target was the mean observed prevalence in 2014–2022, the most recent period without trend breaks. — [ten Dam et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0327264)
- **ten Dam 2025: results and trade-off.**
  - After calibration, HED prevalence was 8.9% predicted vs 8.8% observed (error <0.1 pp), and excessive drinking 8.6% vs 8.5% (<0.2 pp).
  - The cost was distortion of the volume distribution. Mean NABW changed by −8.5% under the excessive-drinking calibration and by <0.1% under the HED calibration; SD changed by −30.8% and −12.5% respectively.
  - The authors recommend the uncalibrated model when the mean NABW itself is the target.
  - Validation was within-sample only (2014–2022).

  — [ten Dam et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0327264)
- **SIMAH: Bayesian history matching.** Parameters are retained if the mean implausibility metric is <3. The alcohol ordinal logistic model converged after 4 waves (mean implausibility 2.39), was calibrated to BRFSS 2011–2015 and validated on BRFSS 2016–2019 (mean implausibility 2.41). Calibration moved the category-intercept odds ratios substantially; for example, the category 3 intercept went from 1,789.76 to 4,002,351.10. — [Kilian et al. 2025 supplement, local _bib copy](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00165-3)
- **SIMAH: rank matching ("deterministic population").** Synthetic individuals are ranked within age, sex, race/ethnicity and education cells and "match[ed] … with a BRFSS individual by rank, assigning alcohol values accordingly". This is a stratified quantile mapping, used to build a pseudo-longitudinal dataset. — [Kilian et al. 2025 supplement](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00165-3)
- **WHO CRA / InterMAHP: consistency rule instead of calibration.** HED prevalence is a survey-modelled input. Volume above the threshold forces HED, and InterMAHP caps P_BAT at the survey P_BD and deflates when the gamma implies more above-threshold drinkers than the survey reports bingers. — [InterMAHP guide](https://www.uvic.ca/research/centres/cisur/assets/docs/intermahp-guide.pdf); [Shield et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00174-4)
- **SAPM v2: calibration on harms, not on HED.** For acute partially attributable conditions, RR = 1 below a peak-day threshold of 3 units for women and 4 for men. The slope above the threshold was fitted by OLS so that the implied AAF matched the observed AAF in each age/sex subgroup. — [Brennan et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10479-013-1451-z)
- **NESARC Markov model: external validation.** The Markov model for HED-frequency states, applied for 11 simulated years to NESARC I, reproduced NESARC III state proportions within 2.2 pp (within 1 pp for volume categories). — [Puka et al. 2022, Addiction (PMC full text)](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- **Copulas.** No alcohol microsimulation using a copula to join volume and HED was found. Copula regression has been used for the joint quantity–frequency distribution (a New Zealand thesis) and in panel models of excessive drinking. These come from a web-search summary and were not opened. — [Massey University thesis (search result)](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/bitstream/10179/16690/1/BoBRomeo2019.pdf); [arXiv 1706.03953](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.03953)

### Inferences
- The cheapest fix consistent with the literature is a stratum-specific intercept (offset) shift on the logit. Solve for δ_sa such that the mean of logit⁻¹(η_i + δ_sa) over *simulated* drinkers in the sex × age stratum equals the survey HED prevalence among current drinkers in that stratum. The volume slope is left untouched.
  - This mirrors ten Dam's choice of calibrating to the target prevalence, but leaves the volume distribution alone.
  - It is a one-dimensional root-find per stratum.
  - It should be validated on held-out ENPG waves, as SIMAH does with its validation period.
- A more structural alternative is the InterMAHP/WHO rule:
  1. Set HED = 1 deterministically for drinkers with simulated volume ≥ the threshold in g/day.
  2. Assign the remaining stratum prevalence among below-threshold drinkers, keeping the logistic shape on volume and using an offset to hit the target.

  This gives coherence with the WHO RR functions, which assume that rule (see Q6).
- Quantile mapping, SIMAH-style rank matching, is attractive if the gap comes from volume-distribution mismatch. Map each simulated drinker's volume rank to the survey volume quantile before evaluating the HED logistic model.
- Whatever the method, report the trade-off ten Dam documents. Calibrating to HED can distort mean or SD volume if done through the volume distribution; offset calibration avoids that.

### Gaps
- No published validation of an *intercept-only* HED recalibration in an alcohol microsimulation was found (the method is standard in prediction-model updating, but no alcohol-specific citation was retrieved).
- No published copula-based volume–HED assignment for alcohol microsimulation was found.

## 4. Longitudinal persistence of HED: tracking, transitions by age, and whether models give HED its own persistence

### Takeaway
HED is moderately persistent at the individual level. In a national US cohort, 72–98% of people reporting HED (depending on frequency category) are predicted to still report HED after 1 year, and 42–63% after 5 years. Persistence is lower in young adults, and there is substantial churn across decades. Among the reference models, none in the verified material gives HED its own state with its own persistence. SAPM v2 ties peak-day drinking to mean volume; SIMAH (Kilian 2025) has no HED; ten Dam's model is cross-sectional each year. The SIMAH team did, however, publish HED-frequency transition probabilities intended for microsimulation.

### Cited Findings
- **Puka 2022: design.** NESARC waves I–II (2001–02 and 2004–05), n = 34,165 US adults. A continuous-time multi-state Markov model was fitted for HED frequency, defined as 4+ drinks (women) or 5+ (men) in the past 12 months. States: non-drinker; drinker without HED; occasional HED (<monthly); monthly HED; weekly HED. Only adjacent transitions were allowed, with covariates age, sex, race/ethnicity and education. — [Puka et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- **Puka 2022: stability.** Non-drinkers: 99% no HED at 1 year and 93% at 5 years. Drinkers without HED: 90% still without HED at 1 year and 78% at 5 years. HED reporters: "72–98% and 42–63% were predicted to continue engaging in HED by the 1- and 5-year follow-up, respectively". No-HED and weekly-HED were the most stable states. — [Puka et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- **Puka 2022: who changes.** Women were less likely to *initiate* HED. Younger adults were more likely both to enter occasional HED and to stay in it. Lower education meant less stable patterns overall but a lower probability of leaving weekly HED. — [Puka et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- **Puka 2022: volume categories for comparison.** The probability of remaining in the same state for 1 year was 90.1% for non-drinkers, 90.2% for category I, 31.8% for category II and 52.2% for category III. — [Puka et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16024)
- **Jefferis 2005 (1958 British birth cohort, n = 8,520).** Binge was defined as ≥10 units per occasion for men and ≥7 for women, derived as last-week units divided by usual frequency. Prevalence at ages 23/33/42 was 37/28/31% in men and 18/13/14% in women. "Most binge drinkers in adulthood changed drinking status." Bingeing at 23 nonetheless predicted bingeing at 42: OR 2.10 (1.85–2.39) in men and 1.56 (1.29–1.89) in women. — [Jefferis, Power, Manor 2005, Addiction](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2005.01034.x)
- **Holahan 2025 (MIDUS).** Among moderate-volume drinkers, baseline binge drinking raised the odds of binge drinking 9 years later more than 6-fold. — [Holahan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10826084.2025.2570882)
- **Models that tie HED to volume.**
  - SAPM v2 derives peak day from mean consumption with linear binge models by age, sex and drinker type, so peak changes only when mean changes. — [Brennan et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10479-013-1451-z)
  - ten Dam predicts HED each year from current NABW, with no individual HED state carried forward. — [ten Dam et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0327264)
  - SIMAH (Kilian 2025) carries only volume category, through an ordinal logistic model with category at t−1 as predictor, and g/day redrawn only when the category changes. — [Kilian et al. 2025 supplement](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00165-3)
- **Chile: no adult HED panel found.** A Chilean university cohort (n = 896, seven waves, July 2020 to April 2021) measured HED repeatedly. It found no change in HED with COVID-19 lockdown, while weekly grams rose by 15.6 g/week. — [Drug Alcohol Rev 2026, DiD study](https://doi.org/10.1111/dar.70229)

### Inferences
- The Chilean model's persistent latent propensity (its own HED persistence) goes beyond the reference models. Puka 2022 supplies an external benchmark: annual HED persistence of roughly 0.72–0.98, depending on frequency, compared with about 0.90 for staying non-HED. The simulated 1-year and 5-year HED-retention rates can be checked against these numbers, with the caveat that they are US data with a 12-month, 4+/5+ definition.
- A fully persistent latent uniform (u fixed for life) combined with a volume-driven probability yields year-to-year HED persistence that is too high for young adults. It can also produce systematic loss of HED when volume falls with age. A partially persistent latent variable (for example an AR(1) on the logit scale, or re-drawing u with probability 1−ρ each year) can be tuned to Puka's retention rates.
- Because Puka's HED Markov model was externally validated (within 2.2 pp over 11 years), it is a defensible template if HED is ever modelled as its own state rather than as a function of volume.

### Gaps
- No Chilean panel estimates of HED persistence were found; whether EPS or other Chilean panels measure HED was not verified.
- No year-to-year HED transition data were found from HILDA, Understanding Society or PSID (search returned none).
- The exact retention values for each HED frequency state (beyond the 72–98% and 42–63% ranges) are in Puka 2022's figures and supplement, which were not extracted.

## 5. Definitional harmonization across waves: 5+/4+ vs 6+ (AUDIT item 3), "more than" vs "or more", 30 days vs 12 months, standard drink size in Chile, and handling series breaks

### Takeaway
HED definitions differ in four ways: drinks threshold, grams per drink, reference period, and whether the threshold is sex-specific. These differences produce breaks large enough to matter at the size of the Chilean gap. In the BRFSS, lowering the women's threshold from 5 to 4 drinks raised women's prevalence by about 2.6–2.9 pp. Two documented ways of handling breaks are:
- covariates for threshold and timeframe in the prevalence regression (WHO/Manthey/Shield);
- step-function trend-break terms plus calibrating only to a break-free window (ten Dam).

Chile's ENPG measures "embriaguez" as 5+ drinks for men and 4+ for women per occasion in the past month. The grams per "trago" are uncertain: 13 g in AUDIT usage, while about 16 g was observed and recommended by MINSAL.

### Cited Findings
- **WHO/GSRAH definition.** HED is ≥60 g pure alcohol on at least one occasion in the past 30 days, reported among the total population or among drinkers only. This is roughly 6 standard drinks of 10 g. — [WHO GHO indicator page (web-search summary)](https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/indicators/indicator-details/GHO/heavy-episodic-drinking-(youth-15--19-years)-drinkers-only-past-30-days-(-)); [Shield et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00174-4)
- **WHO/Shield handling of breaks.** Shield 2025 and Manthey 2019 include survey-level covariates for the HED threshold (reference ≥60 g/occasion) and for the timeframe (reference past month or 28 days) in the HED fractional response regression. — [Shield et al. 2025 appendix](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00174-4); [Manthey et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)32744-2)
- **BRFSS 2006 change.** BRFSS lowered the women's threshold from ≥5 to ≥4 drinks. Women's binge prevalence rose from 7.3% (2004–05 pooled) to 9.9% (2006–07), +2.6 pp or a 35.6% relative increase. The share of women reporting exactly 4 drinks in 2006 (3.6%) was similar to the 2005→2006 jump (2.9 pp). The authors concluded the change was "primarily due to the change in the definition". — [Chavez et al. 2011, Am J Prev Med (PMC; web-search summary)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3090660)
- **Dutch Health Survey breaks.** The Dutch survey had breaks in 2010 (questions changed or moved), in 2012 (women's HED threshold changed from 6 to 4 drinks) and in 2014 (questions changed or moved). ten Dam modelled these with Heaviside step terms interacted with sex, and calibrated only to 2014–2022. — [ten Dam et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0327264)
- **Spanish surveys (web-search summary).** The Spanish national health survey uses 6 units for men and 5 for women within 4–6 hours, while EDADES and ESTUDES use 5 for men and 4 for women within 2 hours. A review notes there is no consensus on the operational definition of quantity, occasion or reference period. — [Rev Esp Salud Pública review (search summary)](https://ojs.sanidad.gob.es/index.php/resp/article/view/685)
- **Chile ENPG (SENDA): "embriaguez".** Defined as 5 or more "tragos" per occasion for men and 4 or more for women. The 2018 questionnaire asks the question sex-specifically ("¿Cuántas veces tomó 5 [4] o más tragos en una ocasión?") with a drink-equivalence card. Results are reported among past-month drinkers. — [ENPG 2016 report (search summary)](https://www.senda.gob.cl/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/InformeENPG2016.pdf); [ENPG 2018 questionnaire (search summary)](https://www.senda.gob.cl/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/Cuestionario-ENPG-2018.pdf)
- **Chile ENPG: the published series.** Embriaguez among past-month drinkers, 2012–2024: 52.1, 43.7, 51.1, 56.3, 50.2, 50.7, 47.2%. The 2014→2016 jump (43.7→51.1) is not explained in the documents found. — [ENPG 2024 press presentation (secondary host; search summary)](https://psiconecta.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/ENPG-2024-Prensa.pdf)
- **Chile ENPG: past-month drinking in 2024.** 34.6% (39.2% in 2022), with fieldwork n = 18,668 aged 12–65. — [Government press release, Dec 2025 (search summary)](https://interior.gob.cl/noticias/2025/12/04/consumo-de-alcohol-alcanza-su-nivel-mas-bajo-en-30-anos-y-marihuana-en-adolescentes-llega-a-minimo-historico/)
- **Other SENDA surveys.** A SENDA study of people aged ≥60 used a lower cut-off: 3+ drinks for men and 2+ for women in the past month. — [SENDA 60+ study (search summary)](https://www.senda.gob.cl/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Estudio-consumo-60-anos-y-mas.pdf)
- **Chile: grams per drink.** In the AUDIT a "trago" is 13 g of pure alcohol: about a 333 ml can of 4.8° beer, 140 ml of 12° wine, or 40 ml of 40° spirits. In the second National Health Survey (ENS, AUDIT plus quantity items), the estimated average content per drink was 16 g, mainly because of glass size and mixed drinks. MINSAL recommended assuming 16 g. — [Castillo-Carniglia 2013 thesis, local _bib copy](https://doi.org/10.1093/alcalc/agt066)
- **Chile: AUDIT item 3 in AAF estimation.** Castillo-Carniglia's Chilean AAF work used AUDIT item 3 (6+ drinks per occasion, past year) for the IHD binge adjustment, and days with "5, 6 or more" drinks for the volume correction. — [Castillo-Carniglia 2013 thesis](https://doi.org/10.1093/alcalc/agt066)
- **Chile: youth HED frequency.** Chilean school-survey analyses built a 4-level HED frequency variable (none, 1–2, 3–9, ≥10 episodes in the past 30 days). — [Prices, alcohol use initiation and HED among Chilean youth, Addiction 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.15167)
- **Chile: HED and SES.** In Chile (2009–10, ages 30–64), HED was equally distributed across SES groups, unlike Finland. — [Drug Alcohol Depend 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2016.12.014)
- **OECD and SAPM thresholds.** The OECD SPHeP threshold is 60 g for men and 48 g for women. SAPM's binge cut-off is >64 g for men and >48 g for women. — [SPHeP-NCDs doc (search summary)](http://oecdpublichealthexplorer.org/ncd-doc/risk_factors/alcohol.html); [Brennan et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10479-013-1451-z)

### Inferences
- **ENPG thresholds in grams.** The 5+/4+ "tragos" thresholds correspond to:

  | g per trago | Men (5+) | Women (4+) |
  |---|---|---|
  | 12 | 60 g | 48 g |
  | 13 | 65 g | 52 g |
  | 16 | 80 g | 64 g |

  For men at 12 g/drink, the ENPG threshold coincides with WHO's 60 g; for women it is below WHO's 60 g, at the OECD/SAPM level of 48 g. The HED flag fed to WHO RR functions should state which threshold it represents. Using the women's 4+ survey flag with WHO HED RRs, which were defined at 60 g, mixes definitions.
- **"More than" vs "or more".** Reading "5 or more" as "more than 5" (or the reverse) shifts the threshold by one drink. The BRFSS evidence (the women at exactly 4 drinks, about 3.6%, ≈ the 2.9 pp jump) suggests a one-drink shift can move prevalence by a few percentage points. That is of the same order as the 4.5 pp gap, so the simulation and target definitions must match exactly.
- **Recommended break handling for ENPG.** Either (a) include wave-period step terms (by sex) in the HED logistic model and calibrate to a break-free window, following ten Dam, or (b) harmonise to a common gram threshold using the drink-count distribution where the microdata allow. For the 2014→2016 jump, check the questionnaires before treating it as real change.

### Gaps
- The ENPG HED item wording and thresholds for waves before 2016, and whether the women's threshold changed at any point, could not be verified from primary SENDA documents (blocked). The 2014→2016 jump is unexplained.
- No published conversion factors between 30-day and 12-month HED prevalence, or between "more than" and "or more" wordings, were found. WHO/Shield use regression covariates, but the coefficients were not visible in the text read.
- No official current MINSAL/SENDA standard-drink definition in grams was retrieved directly. The 13 g and 16 g figures come from the 2013 thesis. The project's canonical handoff (`../../__andres_control/codex_handoff_adam_rr_full_override_caveman.md`) discusses drink-size sensitivity separately.

## 6. How HED enters relative-risk functions for mortality (injuries, IHD, others) in WHO/Shield 2025, GBD and InterMAHP, and implications for a microsimulation (person-level flag vs frequency)

### Takeaway
In WHO CRA, a *binary* past-30-day HED flag (≥60 g on at least one occasion) modifies three outcomes:
- **IHD and ischaemic stroke:** the cardioprotective RR is removed (RR = 1) for HED drinkers below 60 g/day.
- **Injuries:** a multiplicative HED term is added at the same volume — about ×2.61 for road injury and ×1.91 for other injuries.
- **Above 60 g/day:** every drinker is treated as HED.

InterMAHP uses the same IHD/IS rule (RR = max(RR, 1)) and NHIS-derived injury binge factors of 1.48–1.70. GBD uses volume only, which Shield 2025 calls problematic for injuries. A person-level binary flag therefore matches the WHO RR structure. HED frequency would require different, non-WHO risk functions, for example per occasion.

### Cited Findings
- **WHO IHD rule.** "The relative risk is 1 for all people, who consume on average less then 60g of pure alcohol per day, but have at least one heavy drinking occasion per month." Former drinkers have RR 1.25 (men aged 15–34 shown); the IHD function itself comes from Rehm 2016, based on Roerecke & Rehm 2012. — [Shield et al. 2020 appendix, IHD table, local _bib copy](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(19)30231-2)
- **WHO road-injury function.** Non-HED drinkers: RR = exp(0.0029955·x). HED drinkers: RR = exp(0.0029955·x + 0.95935), an HED multiplier of e^0.959 ≈ 2.61 at equal volume (my calculation). Var(b2) = 0.2279. Former drinkers have RR = 1. — [WHO/Europe 2025 injuries web annex (2019 estimates), local _bib copy](https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/383872); identical in [Shield et al. 2020 appendix](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(19)30231-2) (source cited there: WHO 2018)
- **WHO non-road-injury function.** Non-HED: RR = exp(0.0019980·x). HED: RR = exp(0.0019980·x + 0.64710), a multiplier of ≈1.91 (my calculation). Var(b2) = 0.1551. — [WHO/Europe 2025 injuries annex](https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/383872)
- **WHO AAF structure.** The HED/non-HED split enters the AAF only for ischaemic stroke, IHD and injuries (Formulas S4–S5). — [Shield et al. 2025 appendix](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00174-4)
- **InterMAHP rule for IHD and ischaemic stroke.** RR_BD(x) = max(RR(x), 1), which removes protection for bingers, consistent with WHO GBD and GSRAH practice. — [InterMAHP guide](https://www.uvic.ca/research/centres/cisur/assets/docs/intermahp-guide.pdf)
- **InterMAHP injury binge factors.** Binge factors are the ratio of bingers' to non-bingers' risk at the same average consumption: 1.49 for motor-vehicle collisions, 1.70 for intentional injuries and 1.48 for unintentional injuries. They were estimated from linked NHIS drinking–mortality data (n = 134,237). — [InterMAHP guide](https://www.uvic.ca/research/centres/cisur/assets/docs/intermahp-guide.pdf)
- **Roerecke & Rehm 2010: the evidence behind the IHD rule.** Irregular heavy drinking occasions (>60 g or ≥5 drinks per occasion at least monthly) compared with regular moderate drinking gave a pooled RR of 1.45 (95% CI 1.24–1.70) for IHD. The pool was 14 studies, 31 estimates and 4,718 events, with I² = 53.9%. The authors conclude "the cardioprotective effect … disappears" with irregular heavy drinking. — [Roerecke & Rehm 2010, Am J Epidemiol](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwp451)
- **Shield 2025 vs GBD.** "IHME GBD basing injury outcomes on the average level of drinking is potentially problematic, as injuries are strongly linked to HED." WHO estimated 723,500 alcohol-attributable injury deaths in 2019 against IHME's 187,900; for road injuries the figures were 297,500 vs 45,400. — [Shield et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(25)00174-4)
- **SAPM v2 alternative.** Acute harms are a function of peak-day consumption only. The RR is a two-part linear function, flat at 1 below 3/4 units (women/men), with a slope calibrated to observed AAFs. Crime and absenteeism are also linked to peak consumption. — [Brennan et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10479-013-1451-z)
- **INTERSTROKE (case-control, 32 countries).** HED, defined as >5 drinks on ≥1 day per month, was associated with all stroke (OR 1.39, 1.21–1.59), ischaemic stroke (OR 1.29, 1.10–1.51) and ICH (OR 1.76, 1.31–2.36). — [Smyth et al. 2023, Neurology](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000201388)

### Inferences
- **The binary flag is the right exposure for WHO RRs.** A person-level binary HED flag, past 30 days at ≥60 g, is exactly the exposure the WHO RR functions need. Frequency adds nothing within the WHO framework. To be coherent with WHO Formula S5, the microsimulation should force HED = 1 for anyone with average volume ≥60 g/day.
- **Size of the injury impact.** Under-predicting HED by about 4.5 pp among current drinkers moves people from the HED to the non-HED injury function, and the injury RR at a given volume falls by a factor of about 2.6 (road) or 1.9 (other injuries) for those people. Injury AAFs and their policy PIFs will be underestimated roughly in proportion to the missing HED mass at low volume, which is where most HED reporters are (Q2).
- **Size of the IHD/IS impact.** The same under-prediction keeps the cardioprotective RR < 1 for drinkers who should have RR = 1. This makes the IHD and ischaemic-stroke AAFs more negative, i.e. it overstates protection.
- **When frequency would matter.** A frequency variable (occasional, monthly or weekly HED, as in Puka 2022) would matter only with per-occasion or peak-based acute risk functions (SAPM-style). WHO publishes no frequency-specific HED RRs. If frequency is kept for persistence modelling, it should be collapsed to the binary 30-day flag before the RR step.
- **Policy sequence.** In the Kilian/SIMAH sequence (participation → elasticity → continuous volume → recode categories), the HED flag has to be re-derived after the volume change, because WHO requires HED = 1 at ≥60 g/day. If the propensity rule makes HED respond to volume, its implied elasticity of HED should be reported. The literature reviewed gives no HED-specific price elasticity: SAPM could not estimate a peak-day elasticity.

### Gaps
- The current Shield 2025 RR tables for injuries and IHD with HED were read from the 2020 appendix and from the WHO/Europe 2025 annex (2019 estimates). That the 2025 global appendix uses identical coefficients was not checked line by line.
- The GBD 2021/2023 alcohol methods appendix was not opened, so "GBD uses volume only" relies on Shield 2025's description.
- No WHO-endorsed HED-frequency RR functions were found, and no source was found for HED-specific modification of other causes (e.g. haemorrhagic stroke, atrial fibrillation) in WHO CRA beyond IHD, ischaemic stroke and injuries.
